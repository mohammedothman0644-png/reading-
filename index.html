TEACHING READING

01. Introduction to Teaching Reading

Reading is one of the four main language skills, together with writing, listening, and speaking. It is a receptive skill because learners receive information and understand messages through written language.

Teaching reading is not simply asking students to read aloud or translate every word. It means helping them understand texts, find information, recognise main ideas, understand vocabulary in context, and use different reading strategies.

Key idea: A good reading lesson has a clear purpose. Students may read to find specific information, understand the general meaning, study language, or enjoy a text.

02. Reasons for Reading

2.1 Study and Work

Reading supports education and future careers. Students read textbooks, academic articles, instructions, reports, emails, and professional documents. University students use textbooks to prepare for exams; doctors read professional information; employees read workplace reports.

2.2 Pleasure and Choice

People also read because they enjoy stories, novels, magazines, blogs, or articles. Allowing students to choose texts that interest them increases motivation and helps build positive reading habits.

2.3 Language Development

Regular reading introduces vocabulary and shows grammar in real situations. It builds familiarity with tenses, sentence structures, punctuation, expressions, spelling, and how words work together.

2.4 Models for Writing

Reading shows learners how writers organise ideas, construct sentences, develop paragraphs, and connect ideas. An essay, for example, models an introduction, main points, examples, and a conclusion.

2.5 Knowledge and Curiosity

Reading introduces cultures, countries, history, science, and people's experiences. Interesting texts encourage students to ask questions, express opinions, and join discussions.

03. Why Reading Matters in Language Learning

3.1 Vocabulary grows in context

Students meet words in meaningful situations and can often guess meaning from nearby clues rather than memorising isolated words.

“The weather was freezing, so we stayed inside.”
Freezing means extremely cold. Reading also teaches collocations such as make a decision and heavy rain.
3.2 Grammar becomes visible

Texts show how grammar works naturally: past tense for past events, present tense for general facts, linking words to connect ideas, and pronouns referring to previously mentioned people or things.

3.3 Fluency develops over time

Fluency is reading smoothly, accurately, and with less effort. Regular reading makes common words, expressions, and sentence patterns easier to recognise.

3.4 Critical thinking is practised

Readers distinguish facts from opinions, identify a writer's purpose, compare ideas, evaluate information, and make evidence-based conclusions.

3.5 Reading can build confidence

Suitable texts let learners experience success. A beginner who understands a short graded story may gradually feel ready for longer or more challenging texts.

04. Different Kinds of Reading

READ MORE

4.1 Extensive Reading

Reading large amounts of material, usually for pleasure, interest, or general understanding. Students may read novels, stories, magazines, newspapers, websites, and blogs.

  • Learner choice: choose texts that match interests and level.
  • Accessible language: understand without checking every word; graded readers can help.
  • Regular routine: read frequently to build a habit.
  • Light accountability: diaries, short reviews, and discussion—not constant testing.
  • Teacher's role: encourage, recommend, and provide materials.

Success: regular reading, enjoyment, engagement, and general understanding.

READ CAREFULLY

4.2 Intensive Reading

Careful study of a relatively short text to understand details, vocabulary, grammar, organisation, and meaning. It often happens in class with teacher guidance.

Three stages

  1. Before reading: introduce the topic, activate prior knowledge, ask questions, examine title or pictures, and predict.
  2. During reading: identify main ideas and supporting details, use context for vocabulary, and notice grammar and linking expressions.
  3. After reading: summarise, answer questions, discuss the message, or support interpretations with evidence.

Avoid analysing everything at once or translating word by word. Teach learners to use context, word parts, and surrounding information.

Remember: Extensive reading develops reading habits and general fluency. Intensive reading develops careful understanding and language awareness. Both are necessary.

06. Reading Levels: Matching Text and Reader

Reading level is the difficulty of a text and how suitable it is for a learner. A text that is too difficult may discourage students; one that is too easy may offer too little challenge.

6.1 Proficiency

The learner's current language ability. Beginners may understand a short story with simple sentences but struggle with a complex academic article.

6.2 Background Knowledge

Information and experience learners already have. Someone familiar with football may understand a match report more easily than an unfamiliar scientific text.

6.3 Interest and Purpose

Interesting topics increase motivation. Teachers should also consider why students are reading and what they should learn.

6.4 Support

Help can include key vocabulary, topic explanations, pictures, guiding questions, and pair work.

6.5 A Productive Challenge

Choose a text that is slightly challenging but still understandable. Balance learners' current abilities with opportunities to grow.

07. Authentic Texts and Adapted or Graded Texts

7.1 Authentic texts

Texts originally written for real-world readers, not specifically for language learners.

Examples: newspapers, menus, public signs, advertisements, websites, letters, and workplace documents.

Advantages: natural vocabulary, expressions, sentence structures, and realistic communication.

Challenge: idioms, cultural references, difficult vocabulary, and complex sentences may be hard for beginners.

7.2 Adapted or graded texts

Texts written or modified for a particular language level or learning purpose.

Writers may simplify vocabulary, shorten sentences, reduce grammatical complexity, or add explanations. For example, a novel may be rewritten for intermediate learners.

Advantages: supports independent reading and reduces the need for constant teacher help.

Important: good adaptation preserves the main message and natural communication as much as possible.

Main difference: Authentic texts are made for real-world communication; graded texts are adjusted to suit learners' abilities. Select according to level and lesson objectives, then gradually introduce more challenging authentic materials.

08. Core Reading Skills and Strategies

Readers do not need to read every text in the same way. They choose strategies according to their purpose.

8.1 Skimming — get the general idea

Read quickly to understand the topic or main idea. Look at the title, headings, opening sentences, and repeated words; do not focus on every detail.

Example: Look at an article's title and first paragraph to understand its general subject.
8.2 Scanning — find specific information

Move your eyes quickly through a text to find information you already know you need, such as a date, phone number, name, price, or location.

Example: Search an advertisement for the price of a product.
8.3 Reading for Detail — understand accurately

Read carefully and pay attention to sentences, explanations, examples, and supporting evidence. Important for academic reading and examinations.

8.4 Inferring — understand implied meaning

Use clues in the text and background knowledge to understand something the writer does not state directly.

“Ali entered with wet clothes and an umbrella.” We can infer that it was probably raining.
8.5 Guessing from Context — work out an unfamiliar word

Use surrounding words, examples, explanations, contrasts, word parts, and the general topic to estimate a word's meaning.

“After walking for five hours, the travellers were exhausted and needed to rest.” Exhausted means extremely tired.
8.6 Recognising Structure — understand organisation

Identify topic sentences, supporting details, paragraph organisation, pronoun references, and linking expressions. Notice patterns such as cause and effect or problem and solution.

09. Six Principles of Teaching Reading

Principle 1 — Encourage students to read as often and as much as possible

Regular reading increases familiarity with vocabulary, grammar, expressions, and text types. Encourage both extensive and intensive reading, especially the habit of extensive reading. Recommend stories, magazines, and online articles.

Principle 2 — Students need to be engaged with what they are reading

Engagement means being interested, involved, and mentally active. Choose attractive topics and activities that make students think. A football-related text, for example, may motivate football fans.

Principle 3 — Respond to the content, not just the construction

Students should react to a text's message, not only study grammar and structure. After a story about friendship, they might discuss a character's decision and explain their opinions.

Principle 4 — Prediction is a major factor in reading

Use titles, pictures, headlines, covers, and headings to predict content before reading. This activates prior knowledge and creates interest. Students then read to check their predictions.

Principle 5 — Match the task to the topic in intensive reading

Choose activities that fit the text. A news report may lead to questions about what, where, and why; an argumentative article may lead to identifying the writer's opinion and supporting evidence.

Principle 6 — Good teachers exploit reading texts to the full

Use a text as a starting point for vocabulary, grammar, discussion, summaries, writing, and speaking—not simply read it and move on. A passage about education can lead to discussion, new vocabulary, a summary, and opinions.

10. Reading Sequences

A reading sequence is the set of stages and activities a teacher follows to organise a reading lesson.

10.1 Example One: Sunscreen — Pre-intermediate

  1. Show a picture of people sunbathing. Ask whether sunbathing is safe and how people can protect themselves.
  2. Students read quickly and identify the source: a magazine for teenage girls.
  3. They read again and identify skin, hair, and eye colours, arranging them by vulnerability to the sun.
  4. Study vocabulary such as SPF, freckles, fair hair, and dark-skinned.
  5. Extend learning through personal descriptions, character interviews, or a short talk about sun protection.
Main idea: Move from prediction and general understanding to detailed reading, vocabulary, speaking, and writing.

10.2 Example Two: Campaigns — Intermediate

The text is an online news article about Johnny Chester, a young student who campaigns to save a youth centre called The Grove.

  1. Students preview the text and predict its source and topic.
  2. They read quickly and answer: What is the important news? Why did the council change its mind?
  3. Discuss reactions to the story.
  4. Match vocabulary such as funding, achievement, persuade, and survive with meanings.
  5. Read carefully and correct inaccurate statements.
  6. Role-play a radio discussion in groups of five: a council wants to close the school and build a shopping centre. Students represent the council, school, and radio presenter.
Main idea: Reading can lead naturally to speaking, vocabulary development, discussion, and role-play.

10.3 Example Three: WebQuest — Intermediate to Advanced

A WebQuest is an internet-based learning activity. Students use websites selected by the teacher to research information and complete a task instead of searching the whole Internet without direction.

1. Introduction

Introduce the topic and background information; explain key vocabulary if needed.

2. Task

Explain what students must do and the final product they should create.

3. Process

Students visit selected websites, collect information, and complete assigned work.

4. Evaluation

Evaluate learning, discuss findings, and study useful language.

Book example: A WebQuest about UFOs (Unidentified Flying Objects). Groups investigate different perspectives, including UFO believers and scientific views, then hold a debate and reach a conclusion based on collected information.

Limitations: WebQuests need internet access, sufficient English proficiency, and preparation time.

Main idea: A WebQuest combines reading, research, teamwork, critical thinking, and speaking. It gives reading a real purpose.

11. More Reading Suggestions

11.1 Jigsaw Reading

Different students or groups read different parts of a text or different texts on one topic. They share information to understand the complete story or situation. Example: groups read separate story sections, then exchange information to reconstruct the whole story. Develops comprehension, cooperation, communication, and information sharing.

11.2 Reading Puzzles

Students solve problems by organising or reconstructing written information. Example: arrange paragraphs cut from a story into the correct order, or put a series of emails in sequence. Develops awareness of connections and text organisation.

11.3 Using Newspapers

Match articles with headlines or pictures, compare reports of the same event, choose products or holidays from advertisements, or use articles for letters, opinions, and discussions.

11.4 Following Instructions

Read instructions and put them in order or follow them to complete a task, such as using a machine, operating a public telephone, or installing a printer cartridge. Pictures can be matched with instructions.

11.5 Recipes

Read ingredients and steps to prepare a meal. Match each step with a picture or arrange cooking instructions in the correct order. This connects reading with practical tasks.

11.6 Poetry

Give each student a different line; they read aloud and organise themselves into the poem's correct order without showing their lines. Alternatively, remove words and ask students to predict them from context, gradually giving first letters.

11.7 Play Extracts

Students read dialogue from a play or film and act out the scene. They consider stress, intonation, speed, and pronunciation. This links reading and speaking.

11.8 Predicting from Words and Pictures

Give groups clues before reading and ask them to predict a story. Example clues: “Knock on the door”; “Go away!”; “They find a man the next morning”; “He is dead”; “James is in the lighthouse.” Students compare predictions with the actual text.

11.9 Different Responses

Go beyond ordinary comprehension questions. Turn numbers and facts into a table, graph, or diagram; predict a missing ending; describe characters; imagine situations; identify the writer's attitude; or compare examples of a genre such as advertisements.

12. Encouraging Students to Read Extensively

Four factors help students become regular readers:

12.1 Library 📚

Provide a collection of materials at different levels: novels, factual books, stories, and adaptations of films. Include easier and more challenging options so learners can find suitable texts.

12.2 Choice 🎯

Allow students to choose according to interests, genres, and language levels. One student may prefer mysteries; another may prefer historical novels or science articles.

12.3 Feedback 💬

Give opportunities to share reactions orally or in writing: comment on a favourite character or recommend a book. Avoid making every response a formal report or difficult test.

12.4 Time ⏳

Provide time to read during the course, not only at home. For example, set aside ten minutes in some lessons. Teachers should read too, modelling the value of reading.

Teachers cannot force students to enjoy books. They can create opportunities, provide choice, and encourage reading habits.

13. Final Review: Main Ideas

  • Reading is a receptive skill and an essential part of language learning.
  • It develops vocabulary, grammar, spelling, writing, fluency, knowledge, and critical thinking.
  • Extensive reading focuses on larger amounts of material for enjoyment and general understanding.
  • Intensive reading focuses on studying shorter texts carefully.
  • Teachers should select texts based on proficiency, background knowledge, interest, purpose, support, and productive challenge.
  • Students need skimming, scanning, reading for detail, inferring, guessing from context, and recognising structure.
  • The chapter presents six teaching principles, three reading sequences, and a range of creative classroom activities.
  • Library, choice, feedback, and time support extensive reading.
Central message: Teaching reading is not simply teaching students to understand words. It is teaching them to read effectively, independently, critically, and with enjoyment.
